The Inner Science
Born on a Battlefield

What the Bhagavad Gita teaches about freedom in an uncertain world.

Dr. B. S. Chakravarthy • Professor & HOD, Department of Pediatrics • Andhra Medical College / King George Hospital, Visakhapatnam

తెలుగులో చదవండి ← Human Journey

Arjuna’s bow is slipping from his hand. The people before him are not faceless enemies. They are teachers, cousins, friends and witnesses to his childhood. Two armies are waiting, but the celebrated warrior can no longer trust his body, his judgment or even the role that brought him there. His mouth is dry. His limbs tremble. What would victory mean if it required the destruction of the world that gave victory its meaning?

The Bhagavad Gita begins not with confidence but with collapse. Arjuna does not lack knowledge, courage or skill. He is paralysed by something more recognisably human: the discovery that every available choice carries a moral cost. What should we do when the action we can perform and the action we ought to perform no longer seem identical? Krishna does not solve the crisis by removing uncertainty. He does not promise Arjuna a painless decision, a guaranteed victory or a life without grief. Instead, he changes the level at which the problem is seen. He turns Arjuna’s attention from reward to responsibility, from panic to perception, and from the defence of a separate self towards a wider order of life.

This may be why the Gita has survived repeated changes of language, empire, technology and belief. If it were merely an ancient book about war, it might have remained inside the history of war. If it were only a religious promise of liberation after death, it might speak only to those who already accepted that promise. But the Gita asks a question that belongs to every generation: when outcomes cannot be secured, when fear distorts judgment, and when private interest collides with the larger good, how is a human being to act?

Everyone Has a Kurukshetra

The battlefield makes the Gita uncomfortable, and therefore powerful. Had the teaching occurred in a quiet forest hermitage, far from consequence, it would be easier to admire it as spiritual consolation. But Krishna speaks while armies wait. Time will not stop. Arjuna’s refusal to decide would itself become a decision. Our battlefields usually look different. A family and a medical team may have to decide whether another invasive treatment offers hope or merely prolongs suffering. A civil servant may have to choose between an instruction from above and an obligation to justice. A scientist may ask whether everything that can be created should be created. A teacher may struggle between applying a rule uniformly and responding compassionately to a student’s exceptional circumstances.

We often imagine clarity as the disappearance of doubt. In real moral life, complete information rarely arrives. Every option may involve loss. The clarity offered by the Gita is not clairvoyance about the future. It is the capacity to examine, in the present, what is true, what our responsibility requires, and what hidden fear or self-interest is influencing us.

The familiar slogan “act without expecting the fruit” can therefore reduce the teaching to a caricature. How can a responsible person be indifferent to consequences? A physician must observe outcomes. A farmer must care about the crop. A public official must anticipate how a decision will affect people. The Gita does not ask us to ignore results. It asks us not to hand the ownership of our inner life entirely to them.

What lies within our responsibility is attention, discernment, competence, honesty and effort. We can influence the result; we cannot command the universe to deliver it. This small distinction becomes the beginning of freedom.

Detachment Is Not Indifference

The word detachment can sound cold. It is easily mistaken for withdrawal from relationship, responsibility or pain. Yet the Gita’s detachment is not an exit from life. It is a way of being completely present in action without converting the action into property of the ego. A physician treating a critically ill child wants the child to live and must use every appropriate skill.

But life cannot be ordered to obey. If an adverse outcome destroys the physician’s capacity to function, the next patient will meet a healer who has lost inner balance. Yet if the physician says, “The result was never in my hands,” and uses that truth to conceal negligence, this is not detachment. It is an escape from accountability. Yoga lies on the narrow path between these failures: to act wholeheartedly, to examine mistakes honestly, to learn and return to the work, yet not convert success into “I alone did this” or failure into “I am worthless.”

Such balance is not obtained by reading one verse. It has to be practised in each relationship, ambition, disappointment and loss. When success comes, can we enjoy it without enlarging the ego? When it does not come, can we remain open to correction without collapsing into self - condemnation? Equanimity is not a possession. It is a discipline renewed in the changing conditions of life.

Equality Is the Equal Eye

Equality does not require us to pretend that every person has the same abilities, circumstances or responsibilities. A child and a scientist cannot make the same decisions. A trainee and a leader do not carry identical obligations. Yet none of these differences diminishes the fundamental dignity of a living being. The equal eye matters most when we hold power. Courtesy towards those we need is easy; compassion towards those who need us is the real test. How do we speak to a junior colleague, a patient who cannot pay, a worker without influence, a child unable to defend herself, or a person who publicly disagrees with us? Our response reveals whether equality is a conviction or merely a word.

Equality does not weaken justice. Wrongdoing must be named. Responsibility must be assigned. Hard decisions may be necessary. But we must ask whether we are opposing the wrong or enjoying the humiliation of the person who committed it. Can firmness remain free of cruelty? Can power remain free of arrogance? Can disagreement remain free of hatred? The equal eye lives inside these questions.

Brahman: Wholeness Before Division

To many global readers, Brahman may appear to be a remote metaphysical term, or may be confused with a social identity. In the philosophical world of the Upanishads and the Gita, Brahman is not a caste, image or personal label. It points towards the undivided ground of existence, prior to the boundaries through which the mind organises reality. We need not force this insight into scientific terminology. It can be approached through ordinary facts. I do not breathe independently of trees. My body is not separate from elements forged in ancient stars. My life cannot exist without the labour, affection, knowledge and care of innumerable others. The self that feels like an independent island is, on inspection, a temporary meeting place of relationships.

As an intellectual statement, this changes little. When it descends into perception, purity takes on a different meaning. Purity is not the belief that I am morally superior. It is the willingness to notice when greed, jealousy, fear and self -importance are distorting what I see. Harmony does not mean the absence of difference; it means that different forces can remain in relationship without trying to destroy one another.

When this wholeness is glimpsed, peace is no longer a commodity to be imported from outside. It is what remains when inner division is reduced. From that peace arises a quieter joy; from that joy, freedom. Perhaps this is moksha: not only a destination after death, but liberation here and now from the exhausting demand to defend a separate self at every moment.

The Discipline of One Delayed Word

However vast its metaphysics, the Gita is tested in very small moments: the next word spoken in anger; the impulse to diminish another person’s success; the temptation to use someone’s weakness because we possess authority; the wish to punish when our ego is wounded. At that moment, silence is not defeat. Not every thought arising in the mind is a command. Not every emotion is a verdict. Anger may indicate that something is wrong, but anger need not decide the response. Jealousy may expose an insecurity, but injuring another person cannot heal it.

Silence does not suppress thought. It creates a distance between thought and action. In that distance, we can become a witness: Why has this feeling appeared? What identity is it trying to protect? Does it serve truth, or only my status? The body and mind continuously manufacture a separate identity: my position, my talent, my success, my group, my recognition. They then consume enormous energy defending it. Another person’s advancement feels not like progress for the work but like a subtraction from us. A criticism of our opinion feels like an attack on our existence.

Silence briefly interrupts this machinery. Beneath the search for position, wealth, influence and praise, we may discover a deeper search for stability, completeness and truth.

A Teacher Who Returned the Decision

There is a profound reason to call Krishna Jagadguru, teacher of the world. A great teacher does not make the student’s decision. He does not ridicule uncertainty or push a slogan into the student’s hand. He enables the student to see from a different depth. When Arjuna says that he will not fight, Krishna does not respond with a single order. He examines the crisis through body and self, death and continuity, knowledge and action, devotion and discernment, nature and its qualities, individual duty and universal order. Rather than merely answering Arjuna’s question, he asks him to examine the “I” from which the question is being asked. At the end, the decision is returned to Arjuna: reflect fully, then act as you judge appropriate.

This has implications for modern education. Information is only a small part of teaching. A student may memorise the correct answer yet remain unable to identify the right question in an unfamiliar situation. A young doctor may know a protocol but not know how to reason when two guidelines conflict or when the patient before him does not resemble the average patient in the evidence. A citizen may know the law but never ask whether what is legal is necessarily just.

Krishna’s method reminds us that knowledge is not merely accumulated information. It is a reorganisation of vision. An event first seen as personal defeat can become a test of responsibility; anxiety about controlling the future can become care for the quality of present action. This is also why the Gita does not remain the same book throughout one person’s life. A verse encountered at twenty may disclose something different at fifty. Words read during success change after loss. The text is stable; the consciousness meeting it is not. A living philosophical work does not store final answers. It reopens its questions each time the reader changes.

Desire, Comparison and the Machinery of Discontent

We imagine that once a desire is fulfilled, the mind will rest. More often, the horizon moves. The position we wanted reveals another above it. Wealth offers little security before somebody else’s wealth creates a fresh sense of deficiency. Praise gives pleasure, then generates anxiety about whether the next praise will arrive. Desire itself is not the enemy. Hunger seeks food. Curiosity enlarges knowledge. Ambition can extend human ability. The danger lies in the belief: “Only when this is mine will I become complete.” Once an outcome becomes proof of our existence, we may sacrifice truth, relationships and peace to obtain it.

Digital life accelerates the cycle. We compare the disorder inside our own lives with carefully selected moments from other lives. Their achievement is visible; their fear is not. Their recognition is visible; its cost is not. Our ordinary day begins to appear delayed, inadequate and almost unreal. Equanimity does not kill aspiration. It gives aspiration a cleaner centre. “I must rise above everyone” can become “the best work my abilities permit should be done.” The first requires rivals; the second welcomes collaborators. The first is diminished by another person’s success. The second can recognise that success as part of a larger advance.

Contentment does not mean refusing growth. It means growing without using self-disgust as fuel. It means working not because one’s existence is empty, but because existence itself is an extraordinary opportunity to participate in creation. Effort then ceases to be punishment and becomes contribution.

Playing a Role Without Becoming the Role

Each of us occupies many roles: physician, teacher, parent, official, student, leader, citizen. These roles are necessary for society. Trouble begins when the role becomes the whole self. An official may feel that personal worth disappears when office ends. A parent may feel unnecessary when children begin to make independent choices. A scientist may defend an old theory against new evidence because correction feels like loss of identity. A role designed as an instrument of service becomes armour for existence.

The Gita does not ask us to abandon the role. It asks us to perform it completely without being imprisoned inside it. If I am a physician, I must fulfil that responsibility with the best knowledge available. Yet I must know that my knowledge is incomplete, and accept another person’s insight when it benefits the patient. If I am a leader, I must decide; but I need not convert every dissenting voice into an enemy.

This distance from role creates humility. It permits the sentence “I may be wrong” without dissolving responsibility. To say “everything is illusion” and abandon necessary work is not renunciation. Mature detachment says: this work has come to me and I will do it fully, but the name produced by the work is not what I am. Even mortality looks different from here. Our particular role will end; the body has a limit. Yet honest work, transmitted knowledge, an act of compassion, a just institution, or a person we helped to grow may continue in other lives. We cannot control creation, but we can receive one small part of it, improve it, and pass it forward.

From the Ganges to Walden Pond

The Gita’s journey beyond India is evidence of its unusual ability to cross religious and cultural boundaries. Charles Wilkins published the first English translation in London in 1785, opening a major channel through which Indian philosophical thought entered European and American intellectual life. Henry David Thoreau carried the Gita into his experiment in simple living at Walden Pond. In Walden, he wrote that each morning he would “bathe my intellect” in its philosophy. This was more than an educated fascination with a foreign culture. Indian thought entered his lived inquiry into how little a person needs, whether society’s habits are genuine necessities, and how contact with nature changes consciousness.

In Thoreau’s imagination, the water of Walden met the water of the Ganges. The distance between two civilisations dissolved before a common human question: how can we move beyond the noise of convention and hear what is true?

Gandhi’s Dictionary of Conduct

For Mahatma Gandhi, the Gita was not an ornament on a shelf. During imprisonment in South Africa in 1908, he read the copy he had carried with him, and the encounter strengthened his evolving commitment to satyagraha. In his autobiography, he called the Gita his “dictionary of daily reference”: as he consulted an English dictionary for a word he did not understand, he turned to this dictionary of conduct for the trials of life.

The metaphor is exact. A dictionary does not write the sentence for us; it helps us understand the terms with which we must write it. The Gita did not make Gandhi’s political decisions. It turned non-possession and equanimity into daily ethical questions. How should one meet an insulting official, a former colleague who has become an opponent, a corrupt system, disappointment and suffering without surrendering either truth or humanity?

Gandhi did not read the battlefield as permission for violence. He saw Kurukshetra as the struggle between light and darkness in the human heart. One need not accept every part of his interpretation to see what it reveals about the text: a work that addresses a warrior’s duty could, in another historical situation, supply inner discipline to a movement of nonviolence.

His life demonstrated that equanimity is not weakness. To prevent anger from immediately becoming violence, to endure humiliation without consenting to injustice, and to oppose a system without making hatred the master of opposition are forms of strenuous moral action.

Oppenheimer and the Shadow of Knowledge

The most disturbing episode in the Gita’s global journey belongs to J. Robert Oppenheimer. He learned Sanskrit and read the text before directing the laboratory that built the first atomic bomb. Years after the Trinity test, he recalled its vision of cosmic destruction. This should not be simplified into the false claim that the Gita inspired the bomb. Oppenheimer’s encounter is not a victory story for scripture. It is a moral mirror held before the gulf between knowledge, power and responsibility. A person may understand a secret of nature and transform it into unprecedented force. But “I can do this” does not answer “Should this be done?”

Modern science needs that distinction urgently. Artificial intelligence, genetic editing, autonomous weapons and systems of mass surveillance are fields in which capability may be growing faster than moral maturity. Like Arjuna, we can say that we are merely performing a role. But duty in the Gita cannot be reduced to obedience. It must follow an inquiry that tests motive against truth, self - interest and the welfare of life.

Oppenheimer’s story does not offer a simple endorsement of the Gita. It offers a warning more valuable than endorsement: reading a great text is not wisdom. Wisdom begins when we allow the text to interrogate what we are about to do.

The Gita Must Be Questioned to Remain Alive

A modern reader cannot avoid difficult questions. Does instruction about duty on a battlefield legitimise violence? Can svadharma imprison a person inside a role assigned by birth? Can detachment from results become an excuse to tolerate social injustice? To ask these questions is not to insult the Gita. Respect for a living text does not mean placing it inside glass. It means bringing it into conversation with the ethical knowledge of our time. The history of commentary on the Gita - through devotion, knowledge, action, non -dualism, qualified non-dualism, political resistance and nonviolence - demonstrates that the text has never been confined to a single uncomplicated reading.

If duty means only the role society assigns, it can preserve oppression. But if duty is read together with truth -seeking, equal regard, consequence and the larger good, it may give us courage to challenge the role itself. Refusing an unjust order can be duty. Relinquishing inherited power can be dharma. Changing a system from which we benefit can be selfless action.

The finest respect we can offer the Gita is not repetition of its vocabulary. It is permission for its questions to expose our concealed self-interest.

The Kurukshetra of Future Generations

Every generation has its Kurukshetra. For those to come, it may exist among screens, algorithms, climate disruption and widening inequality. They may possess more information than any earlier civilisation, but information does not guarantee discernment. Their machines will decide quickly; the machines cannot independently decide which human values deserve to govern those decisions.

A young person may compare an ordinary life with millions of perfected images and conclude that existence without recognition is nonexistence. A company may measure and sell human attention. A political system may amplify fear and anger because division is profitable. A technology may simplify action while making responsibility for the action increasingly obscure.

In such a world, the Gita’s central gift is not a prefabricated answer. It is a quality of consciousness from which responsible answers may emerge: pause; observe; examine motive; recognise the dignity of life beyond your group; perform your role skilfully; do not feed vanity with success; do not convert failure into a verdict on your worth; be willing to change when a clearer truth appears.

This is not guidance for religious believers alone. It is relevant to the scientist, doctor, public official, teacher, parent and student - to every person whose action enters the life of another.

The Freedom to Stand Second

The ego imagines leadership only as standing in front. Yet when someone else can lead a worthy effort better than we can, the ability to stand second and support that person completely is also leadership. What matters is not “I” but the work that needs to be done; not private glory but the common good. This freedom is rare. We want to serve, but also want our name attached to service. We want truth to prevail, but prefer it to prevail through our own words. We ask for a good leader, then become uncomfortable when that leader moves ahead of us.

Here the Gita’s selflessness becomes radically practical. Work may happen through us, but it is not our possession. We are participants in a creation vast beyond comprehension. Matter has become life; life has become aware; and in us awareness can consciously contribute to the process that produced it. To participate faithfully in our own small way is a source of profound joy.

It does not require first place.

Peace Is Not the Absence of Feeling

The person of steady wisdom is sometimes imagined as a stone untouched by pleasure or sorrow. But emotional absence is not maturity. Compassion requires the capacity to feel suffering. Love includes the possibility of loss. Justice requires disturbance in the presence of injustice. The question is not whether emotion appears, but whether it acquires total authority over speech and action.

Equanimity does not deny grief. Even within grief, it preserves a small light capable of witnessing it. It does not forbid delight in success; it prevents delight from becoming contempt for others. It does not prohibit anger at injustice; it prevents anger from turning us into the cruelty we oppose. This is wider than the contemporary language of emotional regulation. In regulation we manage a feeling. In witness -consciousness we also examine the self in which the feeling arises. Is anger guarding injured dignity? Is fear protecting an illusion of control? Is jealousy hiding a belief that we are not loved or valued? Such questions do not instantly dissolve emotion, but they reduce its blind sovereignty.

Peace is therefore not a motionless surface. It is like the depth of the sea, stable while waves move above. It does not mean experiencing life less. It means experiencing life fully without losing oneself inside every experience. Such peace does not weaken action. It clarifies which call deserves a response and which noise can be allowed to pass.

Here and Now

As I understand it, this is what Gitacharya Krishna teaches. Do not remain imprisoned by the past. Do not surrender the present to fear of the future. Stand fully in this moment. Search for the truth available in the information before you. Perform the role entrusted to you with all the ability you possess. Consider the welfare of life beyond your own identity. Attend carefully to consequences, but do not make them the owner of your peace.

This understanding leads inward, but not away from the world. It is an inward journey that observes thought, purifies intention, and returns us to action with greater clarity: to feel anger without allowing it to speak for us; to know fear without abandoning necessary action; to possess authority without losing compassion; to receive success without surrendering the equal eye.

Moksha then becomes more than a promise after death. It is release from the fatigue of continually defending identity. It is the balance that does not inflate with every compliment or collapse with every criticism. It is the purity that glimpses harmony behind the divisions manufactured by the mind. This is equality. This is purity. This is balance.

This is clarity. This is freedom. This is moksha. This is the way of life revealed by a teacher who spoke on a battlefield and led the human mind towards peace.

A Personal Dedication

I have written this reflection on the occasion of Sri Krishna Janmashtami to share what I have understood from Krishna’s philosophy and what has inspired me in my own life. Across centuries, millions have drawn strength from this wisdom. Some transformed their lives in solitude; some confronted injustice; others entered compassionate service without making personal identity, recognition or reward its purpose.

I dedicate this essay to all those who make Krishna’s teaching visible not merely through their words, but through the way they live and serve. Jaya Krishna... Krishna... Krishna... Hare!
